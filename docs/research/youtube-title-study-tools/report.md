# Building the local outlier tool nobody sells

Every commercial tool defines an outlier the same way (a video's views divided by a baseline taken from its own channel), and none of them is local-first, transparent about its baseline, or evidenced to offer topic clustering or deterministic title-pattern analysis. The closest open-source project is a young, low-star repo, `pandich93/youtube-niche-finder`, which does median-based outliers and title-trait comparison but needs PostgreSQL and documents no clustering. A custom build on the official YouTube Data API v3 is therefore justified. Copy the ratio-to-median idea, make the baseline configurable and visible, and avoid scraping stacks, copyleft archivers, and any promise that title patterns cause views. The main risk is policy rather than engineering. Google's developer rules limit how long certain statistics may be stored and restrict derived metrics. Treat that as a design constraint to check before building a snapshot history. The evidence base is thin in places: commercial formulas are mostly undisclosed, and the title-effect studies are vendor-authored and correlational.

## Every outlier tool computes the same ratio, so the baseline is the real design choice

vidIQ's help page says its score compares a video's views to the channel's average "within a similar timeframe". Its example is 10,000 views on a channel averaging 1,000, which scores 10x. Its colour tiers are black below 2x, blue 2-5x, purple 5-10x, and red above 10x ([vidIQ support](https://support.vidiq.com/en/articles/9660010-outliers)). 1of10 describes "a simple ratio" of views to a channel's typical views, without saying mean or median or what window ([1of10 review](https://1of10.com/blog/1of10-review/)). Only Subscribr publishes a precise formula: views divided by the average of up to 10 surrounding videos, before and after, rounded to one decimal ([Subscribr help](https://help.subscribr.ai/article/48-what-are-velocity-and-outlier-scores)). Vamos uses the channel's median, shows a score only after 10 posts, and counts a post as an outlier above 2x ([Vamos help](https://intercom.help/getvamos/en/articles/16934858-the-outlier-score)). ViewStats is described by a competitor as using the prior 9 or 10 uploads, and the competitor's own pages disagree on which. ViewStats publishes no methodology I could find ([OutlierKit](https://outlierkit.com/resources/viewstats-chrome-extension/); [Overseer benchmark](https://www.overseeros.com/blog/youtube-outlier-benchmark-report)).

Because almost nobody discloses the baseline, scores from different tools are not comparable. That is the opening for a local tool. The best-argued guidance comes from OutlierKit, a competing vendor, so weigh it accordingly. It recommends the median of recent comparable uploads, with the focal video excluded from its own baseline and separate baselines for Shorts, long-form, and livestreams. It rates confidence by sample size: under 5 videos is very low, 5-9 low, 10-19 moderate, and 20-30 high ([Overseer benchmark](https://www.overseeros.com/blog/youtube-outlier-benchmark-report)). It also warns that a tiny baseline inflates results, since 5,000 views against a two-video baseline of 250 yields a meaningless 20x. It treats 2x as noteworthy but possibly noise, 3x or more as significant, and 20x or more as often driven by non-replicable external factors ([OutlierKit](https://outlierkit.com/resources/outlier-scores/)). It also rejects views-per-subscriber as a score because subscriber counts are rounded and not tied to activity. Subscribr nonetheless shows Views/Sub as a separate reach metric.

The academic literature backs the age problem but not the exact recipe. Szabo and Huberman found early views forecast long-term popularity, and YouTube needed about 10 days of tracking to reach the accuracy Digg reached in 2 hours, because YouTube videos keep being found long after upload ([arXiv 0811.0405](https://arxiv.org/abs/0811.0405)). No peer-reviewed work on channel-baseline outlier detection or on comparing baseline methods turned up. Everything about window size and maturity threshold is vendor convention. The practical implication is a core of tiers by data need, which the notes support as an inference. A views-only version divides a video by its channel's median, with at least 10 videos. Adding publish date lets you exclude immature videos or use views per day. Adding duration lets you split Shorts from long-form baselines. A log-scale robust z-score, `(ln v - median(ln v)) / (1.4826 * MAD(ln v))`, is a standard tail-resistant analogue. It ranks identically to a ratio against the same median, so the ratio can stay the headline and the log form can serve as an optional second view. No YouTube tool documents MAD or percentile ranks.

## Commercial tools sell a hosted index, not an inspectable local corpus

The paid field is vidIQ, 1of10, ViewStats, OutlierKit, Subscribr, Playboard, Social Blade, and TubeBuddy, and all are hosted SaaS. Outlier features run from roughly $15 to $70 a month. 1of10's official page lists Basic at $15 a month (yearly) and Pro at $69, with API and MCP access on Pro; its free plan shows only five outlier results and tracks one channel ([1of10 pricing](https://1of10.com/pricing)). A 1of10 review from July 2026 lists different tiers and says there is no public API, so prices are unstable and the notes flag them to be checked live ([1of10 review](https://1of10.com/blog/1of10-review/)). ViewStats Pro is reported at $49.99 a month ([ViewStats pricing](https://www.viewstats.com/pricing); [Tubefilter](https://www.tubefilter.com/?p=180350)). OutlierKit starts around $49 with API on higher tiers ([OutlierKit](https://outlierkit.com/blog/best-youtube-outlier-finder-tools)). vidIQ gates advanced outlier filters behind paid plans. Filters cover outlier score, views, subscribers, views per hour, length, and publish date ([vidIQ support](https://support.vidiq.com/en/articles/9660010-outliers)). Playboard and Social Blade are rankings and stats sites rather than outlier finders; Playboard's popularity score weights recent views plus likes tenfold and only ranks channels with 1,000 or more subscribers ([Playboard](https://playboard.co/en/about)).

What is worth copying is the filter vocabulary and the UI idea: a ranked outlier list filterable by score, views, date, duration, and views per hour, with color-tiered badges and a one-click "find similar" from a title. Competitor tracking on 1of10 and "Add as competitor" on vidIQ confirm that users expect to curate a channel set. What is worth avoiding is the generative title feature. 1of10's title tool drafts titles from patterns in its outlier database rather than exposing the patterns, and OutlierKit's roundup found no title-pattern analysis in 1of10 or vidIQ ([OutlierKit](https://outlierkit.com/blog/best-youtube-outlier-finder-tools)). Topic clustering and visible title templates are the notes' clearest unaddressed gap, though this rests on vendor marketing and cannot be confirmed as absolute. What a local tool cannot match is their pre-indexed cross-niche search; a user-imported corpus only sees the channels the user adds. It also cannot reconstruct daily velocity unless it stores its own snapshots.

## One open-source repo comes close, and the scrapers are a different bet

`pandich93/youtube-niche-finder` is MIT-licensed, with 18 stars and 280 commits, and was built as an alternative to NexLev and vidIQ. It uses the YouTube Data API v3, PostgreSQL, an MCP server, a worker, and a no-build dashboard. It detects outliers against each channel's median, has a "What outliers share" view (for example, a number or "?" in the title), checks titles against niche patterns, and offers a niche score and semantic search ([repo](https://github.com/pandich93/youtube-niche-finder)). Its clustering method is not documented and its maintenance is unverified, so use it as a reference for outlier and title-trait logic, not as a dependency. Other repos on the GitHub youtube-analytics topic are SEO or generation tools, and one (dav19e/VidIQ-Boosted-Insight-Suite) looks like it may distribute cracked software and should be avoided ([topic page](https://github.com/topics/youtube-analytics)).

For importing, the real fork is official API versus scraping. Scraping stacks are well maintained: yt-dlp (Unlicense, pushed 2026-10-23, release 2026.10.21) and YouTube.js (MIT, v18.3.0) are current, while pytube, youtube-search-python, and the yt-dlp OAuth plugin are dead or archived and scrapetube has had no push since September 2025 ([yt-dlp](https://github.com/yt-dlp/yt-dlp); [YouTube.js](https://github.com/LuanRT/YouTube.js); [scrapetube](https://github.com/dermasmid/scrapetube); [pytube](https://github.com/pytube/pytube)). But they carry operational and policy cost. The yt-dlp wiki says YouTube is gradually requiring PO Tokens, that guest sessions are limited to about 300 videos an hour, and that account use risks a ban ([yt-dlp wiki](https://github.com/yt-dlp/yt-dlp/wiki/Extractors)). The Data API Developer Policies prohibit scraping directly or indirectly ([Developer Policies](https://developers.google.com/youtube/terms/developer-policies)). Since this project has chosen the official API, the quota math is favourable. `videos.list`, `playlistItems.list`, and `channels.list` cost 1 unit per call against a default 10,000 daily units, while `search.list` has its own 100-call daily cap ([Google docs](https://developers.google.com/youtube/v3/determine_quota_cost)). By the notes' own arithmetic, which assumes 50 items per page and was not re-fetched, a full 100,000-video pass costs about 4,000 units. A practical consequence is to resolve a handle to a channel's uploads playlist and page that playlist, avoiding `search.list` entirely. The "weeks" estimate for scraping the same volume is also the notes' calculation, not a cited figure.

For structure, the self-hosted archivers are references only. TubeSync (Django, SQLite default, AGPL-3.0) and Pinchflat (Elixir, SQLite, AGPL-3.0, last push December 2025 and 202 open issues) are the closest single-user SQLite shapes. Tube Archivist needs Elasticsearch, Redis, and about 2GB of RAM and is GPL-3.0 ([TubeSync](https://github.com/meeb/tubesync); [Pinchflat](https://github.com/kieraneglin/pinchflat); [Tube Archivist](https://github.com/tubearchivist/tubearchivist)). None is built for stat snapshots. Copying code from the GPL and AGPL projects would impose copyleft, so copy only ideas such as scheduling, interval throttling, and metadata trimming.

## Topic grouping and title patterns are best built from simple, inspectable parts

For topic grouping, the standard open-source recipe is BERTopic: sentence-transformer embeddings, UMAP, HDBSCAN, then c-TF-IDF labels, with a `reduce_outliers` step for the unassigned "-1" topic ([BERTopic](https://github.com/maartengr/bertopic); [FAQ](https://maartengr.github.io/BERTopic/faq.html)). The surveyed examples apply it to transcripts or to LLM-enriched metadata rather than bare titles. Grootendorst's Kurzgesagt tutorial uses Whisper transcripts, and a 2025 chapter had GPT-4o mini summarize each video from title and description before clustering ([tutorial](https://www.maartengrootendorst.com/blog/whisper/); [UT DSpace](https://dspace.ut.ee/items/b286907b-a431-4104-b94c-a0898fcd8e8d/full), search-summary only). No published evaluation of title-only topic modeling turned up. Expect many unassigned titles. My inference is to embed title plus description, keep topic assignment local, and let the user relabel clusters; this is untested.

For title patterns, handcrafted features are trivial to compute and fully transparent: length, numerals, question marks, capitalization, brackets, and second-person words. Ready-made libraries add little. textstat is healthy (0.7.4, MIT) and VADER is stable but unmaintained since 2020 ([Snyk textstat](https://snyk.io/advisor/python/textstat); [Snyk VADER](https://snyk.io/advisor/python/vaderSentiment)). Hugging Face clickbait classifiers report around 98.6% accuracy but were trained on news headlines, and one Medium experiment found little correlation between clickbait score and YouTube views ([DistilBERT card](https://huggingface.co/ENTUM-AI/distilbert-clickbait-classifier/blob/main/README.md); [Medium](https://medium.com/data-science/youtube-views-predictor-9ec573090acb)). A hybrid of embeddings plus 15 explicit features reached F1 91% on a clickbait task, but code was not located ([arXiv 2602.18171](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2602.18171)). Treat any classifier as one weak feature and validate on a hand-labeled sample.

The evidence on whether title features drive views is weak, which should shape how the tool words its output. OverseerOS, a vendor, found word-list clickbait titles (10.3% of 2,309 videos across 62 channels) had a median of 1.00x against the channel baseline, and 169 matched same-channel pairs gave 1.06x with 52.1% wins ([OverseerOS](https://www.overseeros.com/blog/do-clickbait-youtube-titles-get-more-views)). AIR Media-Tech's correlational study of 18,080 channels reports 30-50 characters best overall, 90+ worse, and niche-dependent effects for numbers, question marks, and ALL CAPS, with no effect sizes and engagement rate rather than views as the metric ([AIR](https://air.io/en/audience-growth/how-to-write-a-youtube-title-that-gets-clicked-research-across-11-niches)). Trending-dataset analyses suffer selection bias because only trending videos appear ([TDS](https://towardsdatascience.com/youtube-trending-video-analysis-with-pandas-and-seaborn-8eec48a5e2d9/)). The sound pattern is within-channel normalization and matched same-channel comparison, because raw cross-channel correlations mostly measure channel size. The tool should show "what outliers share" as descriptive lift over the channel's own non-outliers, not as advice.

## The API policy may constrain snapshot history more than any engineering choice

The Developer Policies say statistics retrieved without user authorization, such as subscriber count, cannot be stored beyond 30 days without channel-owner authorization. View-count-type statistics may be kept longer with re-verification every 30 days. The policies also forbid replacing API data with independently calculated data or creating new derived metrics, and forbid aggregating across content owners ([Developer Policies](https://developers.google.com/youtube/terms/developer-policies)). The notes add that from 2026-06-01 audited developers may apply to create additional metrics or store statistical data. The notes' source for this was a model summary of the page, and its clause numbers should be checked on the primary page before quoting. Read plainly, an outlier score is a derived metric and a local multi-channel corpus is an aggregation, so a personal tool sits in a gray zone. No source on enforcement against individual users was found, and this is not legal advice. The design response is cheap: refresh stored view counts at least every 30 days, record fetch timestamps, avoid long-term subscriber snapshots, and do not redistribute the data.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the question. The outlier math is a solved convention (the ratio), and the differentiation lies in the parts vendors hide or skip: an explicit baseline, a visible sample-size confidence, a format split, and a topic and title-feature layer that is inspectable rather than generative. The main reuse list is therefore short. Reuse the ratio and its 2x, 5x, and 10x tiers, the vidIQ-style filter set, the Data API with uploads-playlist paging, SQLite, and BERTopic or plain TF-IDF for topics. Copy only the ideas from TubeSync and Pinchflat, and use the niche-finder repo as a logic reference. Avoid scraping foundations, dead libraries, copyleft code, and causal-sounding title scores.

Two uncertainties remain open and decide the first spike. First, the best window and maturity threshold are untested, so expose them as settings and compare rankings across a few values on real channels. Second, the Data API policy tension on stored statistics and derived metrics needs a read of the primary page before any snapshot feature is built. Because the notes found no study of title-only clustering quality, a small hand-labeled sample from the user's own channels is the right test of whether the topic layer is useful.

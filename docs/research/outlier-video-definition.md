# Define outliers by channel-relative log views

A local title-study tool should call a video an outlier when its public view count is at least about **3x the median of the same channel's other mature, same-format videos published nearest in time**. A within-channel rank rule ("top 10% of the channel") should back the ratio up, and every score should show its baseline size. This matches the one definition every documented tool shares: views divided by a baseline taken from the channel's other uploads. The tools differ only in baseline statistic, window, age handling and format split ([vidIQ help](https://support.vidiq.com/en/articles/9660010-uncover-top-performers-outliers); [Subscribr help](https://help.subscribr.ai/article/48-what-are-velocity-and-outlier-scores); [Vamos help](https://intercom.help/getvamos/en/articles/16934858-the-outlier-score)). The data the tool can actually obtain (`viewCount`, `publishedAt`, `duration`, rounded subscriber count) is enough for a ratio-to-median score with a maturity gate and a Shorts/long-form split, but not for true age-matched curves. Those would need snapshot history the Data API developer policy appears to forbid keeping beyond 30 days ([Developer Policies](https://developers.google.com/youtube/terms/developer-policies)). Two things recently made scores less trustworthy than the vendor pages suggest. YouTube redefined what counts as a view on 2026-08-24, so cumulative counts now straddle two definitions ([videos resource](https://developers.google.com/youtube/v3/docs/videos)). And the 2x/3x/5x/10x cut-offs have no published statistical validation. This report builds on the earlier tool survey (`docs/research/youtube-title-study-tools/outlier_methods.md`) and the data-access note (`docs/research/youtube-data-access.md`) without repeating their setup, quota and per-tool details. It ends with a concrete definition and the decisions still open.

## Cumulative views, not views per day, with a 30-day maturity gate

The tool will see one cumulative number per video, and the choice is how to make videos of different ages comparable. Three options are available without snapshots. One is to exclude young videos. Another is to divide by age (views per day). The third is to compare only against videos of similar age. The evidence favours the first and third over the second. The peer-reviewed anchor is Szabo and Huberman's study of 7,146 YouTube videos. Log-views early in a video's life predict log-views at day 30 well, with a correlation of roughly 0.92 after about five days on the log scale versus about 0.70 on the raw scale (per a fetched ar5iv full-text summary; the exact wording was not verified) ([ar5iv 0811.0405](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/0811.0405)). The same paper finds that YouTube needed about 10 days of tracking to forecast long-term popularity, and that evergreen content is predicted worse ([arXiv 0811.0405](https://arxiv.org/abs/0811.0405)). A vendor observational study of 77 long-form videos found the median video already had **about 61% of its day-7 views at 48 hours**, with wide spread (IQR 48-71%) ([OverseerOS](https://www.overseeros.com/blog/the-48-hour-youtube-test)). Together these support 30 days as the best-supported maturity default and 7 days as an absolute minimum. No primary source supports a specific 90-day threshold.

Views per day is the tempting shortcut, and it is the wrong default. Lifetime average views/day divides a front-loaded total by a growing denominator, so it penalises older videos systematically. A vendor study of 726 videos older than a year found they still gained a median of about 480 views/day but ran at only **0.54x, 0.52x and 0.27x of same-channel newer videos** at 1-2, 2-3 and 3+ years ([OverseerOS](https://www.overseeros.com/blog/do-old-youtube-videos-still-get-views)). That sample was winner-heavy by the authors' own admission, so the magnitudes are soft, but the direction fits the front-loading story. The practical consequence is that the baseline pool should contain only videos at least 30 days old. The focal video can still be scored while younger, but it should carry an "immature" flag. Views/day is worth showing as a secondary column for exactly those young videos, labelled as a pace figure rather than the outlier score.

True age matching, which compares a video with its siblings at the same age, is out of reach. Public data gives only the present cumulative count, and the vendor methodology pages say as much ([OutlierKit methodology](https://www.overseeros.com/blog/youtube-outlier-benchmark-report)). The partial substitute is to pick the baseline by publication time rather than by recency alone. Subscribr averages up to 10 surrounding videos published before and after the focal one ([Subscribr help](https://help.subscribr.ai/article/48-what-are-velocity-and-outlier-scores)), and a study tool that scores a channel's whole history should do the same with the K nearest-in-time other videos. That choice also handles drift, since a channel's normal level changes as it grows. Trailing windows fail for the oldest videos, which have no earlier siblings. None of the documented tools age-match, and all of them use a recent window with the focal video left out.

## Median of log views, same format, at least five to ten neighbours

Every documented formula uses the median, not a trimmed mean or MAD. Examples are the Apify actors (median of about 15 recent uploads for datascoutlab, about 30 long-form uploads for vanity_arias) ([datascoutlab](https://apify.com/datascoutlab/youtube-outlier-finder.md); [vanity_arias](https://apify.com/vanity_arias/youtube-outlier-finder.md)), Vamos ([help](https://intercom.help/getvamos/en/articles/16934858-the-outlier-score)), and TubeRecon (median of 9 prior videos) ([Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/hbgdjglflbmbbnnobocoogjefelkgflb), per a search summary). The reason is simple: one viral hit drags a mean baseline up and hides the next outlier. No source compares estimators empirically. The ratio to a median is mathematically a log-difference from the log-median, so ranking by ratio and ranking by log score agree. This is why the tool can display "6.2x" while computing on the log scale.

The one extension worth making is spread awareness. A fixed multiplier produces a channel-dependent false-positive rate. If a channel's log-views have standard deviation sigma, a ratio r corresponds to z = ln(r)/sigma. Under an assumed sigma of 0.8-1.2 (an assumption to measure on the tool's own data, not a sourced figure), 2x sits at z of roughly 0.6-0.9 and flags something like a fifth of a volatile channel's videos, while 10x sits at z of roughly 1.9-2.9. That arithmetic explains why practitioner pages call 2x "noteworthy but may be noise" and treat 3x and above as the working line ([OutlierKit](https://outlierkit.com/resources/outlier-scores/)). Two cheap remedies exist. The first is Eden's approach of pairing the multiplier with a within-creator rank chip that shows only when a post is in the creator's top 10%, which needs 20 or more posts and specifically protects consistently strong creators whose median is already high ([Eden help](https://eden.so/help/discover/outlier-multiplier/)). The second is a robust log z-score, (ln v - median(ln v_others)) / (1.4826 x MAD(ln v_others)), with a floor on the denominator. No tool documents the second; it is a standard method, and with fewer than about 10 neighbours MAD is unstable, so it should stay a secondary column.

Window size and minimum sample follow tool convention, not validated optima. Documented windows run from 9 to 30 videos, with minimum gates of 2 (datascoutlab, too weak), 10 (Vamos hides the score below 10 posts) and 20 for Eden's rank chip. Vendor guidance grades confidence as very low under 5 videos, low at 5-9, moderate at 10-19 and high at 20-30 ([OutlierKit methodology](https://www.overseeros.com/blog/youtube-outlier-benchmark-report)). The same page shows why the gate matters: 90,000 views against a 6-video median of 3,000 gives 30x on weak evidence. For a study tool that wants to use small channels too, a reasonable compromise is K of about 10-20 nearest neighbours, hiding the score under 5, dimming it under 10, and flooring the baseline (datascoutlab uses 1,000 views) so tiny channels do not produce absurd ratios ([datascoutlab](https://apify.com/datascoutlab/youtube-outlier-finder.md)).

Format must be split before any of this is computed. Shorts and long-form differ in scale and dispersion. vidIQ's study of channels' first ten uploads found a median first Short at about 1,362 views at 30 days against about 180 for the first long-form video, and a best-of-ten Short at 6.5x the first versus 10.8x for long-form ([vidIQ Research](https://vidiq.com/research/youtube-first-10-videos-study/)). A smaller blog sample found the reverse median ordering ([Socialcrawl](https://www.socialcrawl.dev/blog/youtube-shorts-vs-long-form.md)), so the evidence on direction is mixed, which is itself the argument for separate baselines. Livestream replays, premieres and upcoming items should likewise be excluded from the pool or baselined separately; upcoming, live and post-live items should never be scored, because their views are still accumulating.

## Subscriber counts are too coarse for the score but useful for filtering

Subscribers can enter the definition in three ways: as the baseline (views per subscriber), as a score modifier, or as a filter and display field. Only the last holds up. The API's `subscriberCount` is rounded to three significant figures and can be hidden (`hiddenSubscriberCount`) ([channels resource](https://developers.google.com/youtube/v3/docs/channels)), and yt-dlp's `channel_follower_count` is a display figure too. Subscriber count also says nothing about how many subscribers actually watch. A vendor test of 1,035 videos found a subscriber-ratio predictor had a median error of about 3x, with only about 29% of predictions within 2x of the real result ([OverseerOS views predictor](https://www.overseeros.com/blog/youtube-views-predictor)); that is vendor data and unreviewed, but it is consistent with OutlierKit's example of a 500k-subscriber channel that normally draws 30k views next to a 40k-subscriber channel that regularly draws 80k ([OutlierKit methodology](https://www.overseeros.com/blog/youtube-outlier-benchmark-report)). A channel-own baseline already captures the channel's real audience, and that is the point of the ratio.

The sensible role for subscribers is context. vidIQ shows subscribers and the channel average next to each result and filters on subscribers, and its help page states plainly that a small channel's 10x (10,000 views against a 1,000 average) outranks a big channel's 1x ([vidIQ help](https://support.vidiq.com/en/articles/9660010-uncover-top-performers-outliers)). For a title study this is a feature, since a topic that wins 10x on a 5,000-subscriber channel is evidence the topic travels beyond an existing audience. The tool should therefore store subscriber count per channel, display it, and offer a subscriber range filter (and perhaps a views-per-subscriber column in the style of Subscribr, which shows it separately without folding it into the score). It should not use subscribers in the outlier score.

## The data sources and the 2026 rules limit what can be promised

Both sources supply the essentials. The Data API gives exact `viewCount`, `publishedAt`, ISO 8601 `duration`, `liveBroadcastContent`, `liveStreamingDetails` (present for upcoming, live or completed broadcasts), `privacyStatus` and rounded channel subscribers. It has no Shorts flag and no documented members-only flag ([videos resource](https://developers.google.com/youtube/v3/docs/videos); [channels resource](https://developers.google.com/youtube/v3/docs/channels)). yt-dlp adds `availability`, `live_status`, `was_live` and `release_timestamp`, but in cheap flat mode it gives parsed display views and only approximate dates ([yt-dlp README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/yt-dlp/yt-dlp/master/README.md)). Shorts need a heuristic. YouTube's help page defines Shorts as videos of up to 3 minutes that are square or vertical, uploaded on or after 15 Oct 2024; earlier uploads keep their old classification and landscape clips stay long-form ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/15424877?hl=en), from a search summary). Duration of 180 seconds or less is therefore a proxy that misclassifies landscape short clips, and the only classification that matches YouTube's own is enumerating the channel `/shorts` tab with yt-dlp flat mode, at the cost of extra scraping (per the data-access note). The 61-180 second band is the ambiguous one and could get a third bucket.

Two policy facts bound the design and the user must decide how to treat them. First, from 2026-08-24 views in all formats count at playback start (first frame, including autoplay and hover), where Shorts had worked this way since 2025-03-31, and long-form used an undisclosed minimum-watch threshold before ([videos resource](https://developers.google.com/youtube/v3/docs/videos); [revision history](https://developers.google.com/youtube/v3/revision_history); [ppc.land](https://ppc.land/youtube-counts-views-from-the-first-frame-across-all-formats-on-august-24/)). No source documents retroactive restatement or the size of the effect. A long-form video's count therefore changes slope after that date, and a recent video scored against a pre-change median is probably biased upward. The tool should record a `definition_epoch` with every stored count and prefer baselines within one epoch, flagging videos published after the change as "new-count" until enough of them exist. Second, the Developer Policies limit storage of non-authorized statistics to 30 days and bar using API data "to create new or derived data or metrics", including scores built from views ([Developer Policies](https://developers.google.com/youtube/terms/developer-policies)). Read literally, that conflicts with both a long snapshot history and persistent stored outlier scores. The safest readings are to recompute scores on demand from fresh counts rather than persist them, or to label them as independently computed. Whether the policy binds a single-user local tool, or a yt-dlp-only one, is not stated anywhere found. That is a ToS decision that belongs to the project owner. The new `videos.batchGetStats` method (1 quota unit per call, no authorization needed for public videos) makes frequent count refresh cheap ([batchGetStats](https://developers.google.com/youtube/v3/docs/videos/batchGetStats)). The YouTube Analytics API cannot help, because it serves only the owner's own channels and linked content-owner channels ([YouTube Analytics API](https://developers.google.com/youtube/analytics)).

## Present a chip with a tier, a baseline and a confidence cue

Presentation across tools has converged on a multiplier chip with colour tiers, a sort by score and a minimum-score filter. vidIQ's confirmed ladder is black under 2x, blue 2-5x, purple 5-10x and red above 10x. Eden uses six tiers (grey under 1x up to red at 50x or more), two decimals under 10x and a tooltip that explains the baseline in plain language ([vidIQ help](https://support.vidiq.com/en/articles/9660010-uncover-top-performers-outliers); [Eden help](https://eden.so/help/discover/outlier-multiplier/)). Eden's top tiers are overkill for a study tool. vidIQ's four bands are enough, and the colours should be paired with the number so they do not carry meaning alone.

Three things the vendors do inconsistently are worth doing deliberately. First, expose the baseline: vidIQ lists the channel average on each result, and the tooltip should read "vs median of N videos". Second, show confidence: Vamos hides scores under 10 posts, and the OutlierKit grades give a tiered cue, so a small "n=" label with dimming under 10 neighbours costs nothing. Third, treat extremes with suspicion without hiding them. OutlierKit advises that a 20x or higher score should first prompt an audit of the baseline and often reflects non-replicable factors like a celebrity mention ([OutlierKit](https://outlierkit.com/resources/outlier-scores/); [methodology](https://www.overseeros.com/blog/youtube-outlier-benchmark-report)). A "possible fluke" marker for an isolated spike, or any score with a small baseline, does that. Negative outliers are the gap. OutlierKit does not highlight scores under 1x and Eden greys them out. A study of what topics fell flat is plausibly useful for title research, so a muted "under" band at 0.5x or lower, off by default and kept out of the outlier count, is a cheap extension. This is my suggestion; no tool documents it. For the actual research purpose the strongest signal is not a single high score but a cluster. OutlierKit's pattern advice (three or more outliers with similar titles, topics or formats) fits the topic-grouping goal, so the tool should count distinct channels with outliers in the same topic group, which is more robust than any one video's multiplier.

## Recommended definition

An **outlier video** is a public, completed video whose view count is at least **3x the median view count of up to 15 other videos of the same format from the same channel**, selected as the nearest in publication time, excluding the video itself. Details:

1. **Eligibility of the baseline pool.** Videos of the same format (Short, long-form, or a separate 61-180s bucket if the heuristic is used), public, not upcoming/live/post-live, and at least 30 days old. Livestream replays and premieres are separate or excluded. Items the source cannot return (members-only, deleted) are skipped, never counted as zero views.
2. **Baseline.** Median of `viewCount` over the K = 15 nearest-in-time pool videos (before and after the focal one), with a floor of 1,000 views. Require at least 5 neighbours to show a score and at least 10 for full confidence.
3. **Score.** `score = views / baseline`, computed on the log scale and displayed as a multiplier (one decimal under 10x, integer above). Also store a robust log z-score and the within-channel percentile as secondary columns.
4. **Flag.** Outlier if `score >= 3` OR the video is in the channel's top 10% (only for channels with 20 or more eligible videos). Tiers: under 2x normal, 2-3x notable (not flagged), 3-5x strong, 5-10x breakout, 10x or more exceptional. Scores of 20x or more, or computed on fewer than 10 neighbours, carry a "check baseline / possible fluke" marker.
5. **Age.** A focal video under 30 days old is scored but marked "immature", with views/day shown alongside. Views/day is never used as the score.
6. **Subscribers.** Never part of the score. Stored and shown per channel, and available as a filter.
7. **Epoch.** Each stored count carries a `definition_epoch` (before or after 2026-08-24). Long-form baselines are drawn from one epoch where enough videos exist.
8. **Display.** Chip with number and colour, tooltip "vs median of N videos (baseline X views)", sort by score, minimum-score filter defaulting to 3, optional "under" band at 0.5x or lower off by default.

## Open decisions for the user

- **Threshold default.** The 3x line is a convention with no validation. Decide whether to keep 3x, lower it to 2x for more volume, or make it a percentile-first rule. The sigma values behind the false-positive arithmetic should be measured on the tool's first real channels, and the default revisited then.
- **Baseline size and window.** Choose K (15 proposed), the minimum neighbours to show a score (5), and the floor (1,000 views). None is validated, and a small simulation on collected data could settle them.
- **Maturity age.** Confirm 30 days as the pool cutoff, or choose 7 for fresher data or longer for evergreen-heavy niches.
- **Shorts classification.** Accept the duration-only proxy (180 seconds or less), or add yt-dlp `/shorts` tab enumeration for accuracy at the cost of scraping. Decide whether to keep a separate 61-180 second bucket.
- **Data source and policy risk.** Decide how to handle the Developer Policies' 30-day cap on stored statistics and the bar on derived scores: recompute on demand, label scores as independently computed, use yt-dlp as the primary source, or accept the risk for a personal local tool. A check of the current YouTube Terms of Service on automated access was not done.
- **View-definition epoch.** Decide whether to baseline strictly within an epoch (cleaner, but thin for recent long-form videos) or accept a mixed baseline with a visible flag. Also decide whether to ignore the effect on the assumption that it is small, since YouTube has published no magnitude.
- **Percentile rule and robust z.** Whether to ship the top-10% rank rule and the MAD-based z-score in the first version or hold them for later.
- **Negative outliers.** Whether to include the muted "under" band at all.
- **Topic-level signal.** Whether the study view should lead with cross-channel clusters (distinct channels with 3x videos in one topic group) rather than individual video scores. That is the likely main use, and it depends on the topic-grouping work.

## Evidence limits

Most view-dynamics and threshold sources are vendor pages with small, self-selected samples, and no tool publishes its complete formula. No peer-reviewed work addresses channel-baseline outlier detection, and no study compares baseline estimators or validates any cut-off. The arXiv 1402.1777 and Cha et al. 2007 numbers were not retrieved. The premiere/`liveStreamingDetails` behaviour, members-only handling, the `videos.list` per-call ID cap and the retroactive treatment of the 2026-08-24 change are unconfirmed in primary text.
